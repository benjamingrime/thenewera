So now that we have the theoretical basis for how models can know things like we do, how do they do this in practice? For us, it's sort of intuitive as we've been using language our whole lives. With machines, it's less obvious. 
So far, I've anthropomorphised these models quite a lot. I'm going to continue to doing that because the parallels are useful.
In some ways, LLMs has a brain like ours: it has neurons and synapses (nodes and weights), and researchers are increasingly trying to adapt AI brains to mimic human brains. It too can learn; "machine learning". First, we need to understand what form the learning material comes in.

## Data
You might have heard that AI needs "good training data". Data is simply recorded information. We learn through information, machines learn through data. For example, I know the names of people in the Manchester office. They're in my head because it's information I've gathered and it's been stored in my brain through neural adaptation. However, it's only data until I record it somewhere. Another example: word documents are data, they are recorded information (it doesn't have to be in Excel to be data). Good training data is simply the correct recorded information, and there's plenty of it on the internet.

LLMs intake data from all kinds of sources: webpages, digital books, transcripts, articles, papers. Combined, their training data amounts to trillions of words. Each word is converted into co-ordinates that can be placed in space, such that "king" is near "queen", "man" is near "woman", and if you drew a line to connect "man" and "king", that line would be almost identical to the one that would connect "woman" and "queen". You could imagine this line representing the concept of "royalty", and this absolutely blew my mind when I learnt about it. This is, for example, how an image generation model knows to add a crown when you ask it for a "royal puppy", or whatever people use image generation models for. This is how it learns how abstract concepts relate to each other, in many-dimensional space.

The brain (a web of trillions of interconnected numbers) then learns the patterns of the data by computing these trillions of words represented as co-ordinates like an enormous calculator. Roughly, a trillion tokens (data) times a trillion connections (brain) equals a septillion multiplications to train a frontier LLM, a number so big that I might as well have just said "lots". When we were in school with our little casio calculators, we'd put a small calculation in like two times two and the little casio would calculate the answer instantly. Then we'd mash the keypad with a really big number and times that by another really big number, and the calculator would have to wait a second before calculating because the numbers are so big. Can you imagine training an LLM with the computational power of a little casio? One septillion multiplications? It would take billions of years because the processor is tiny. ### Data Centres
Simply put, data centres are a big collection of computers and calculators (they all mean the same thing) that perform these septillion multiplications required to train and run an LLM: it's the hardware the brain runs on. Ours run on carbon (and coffee), their's on silicon. More computational power (more *compute*, bigger calculator, the mega-casio) means you can train and run a bigger computer brain, which lets the models learn more sophisticated and abstract patterns in the data (which we might loosely call *intelligence*, maybe). This increase in compute to improve models over time is called *scaling*. Compute has been increasing roughly 5x per year for the same cost. It's also the reason why you should have invested in Nvidia 5 years ago. I thought about doing this but myself, but quickly realised I was a student with no money.

There's also, algorithmic efficiency: the structure of the brain itself. because we can rewire the brain to get the same performance for less energy, we can use the same amount of compute more efficiently to get even better results. this is the reason you've probably heard of DeepSeek. algorithmic efficiency has been increasing 3x per year, i.e. the same energy costs go three times further. software + hardware improvements result in 12x efficiency per year.
