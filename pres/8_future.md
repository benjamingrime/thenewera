### Moloch

A Moloch Trap occurs when small, self-interested groups in a population pursue their own interests to gain a short-term competitive advantage, leading to outcomes that leave the population long-term worse off, including the self-interested group themselves.

Liv Boeree gives the example of a concert, where everyone is assigned seats with a roughly equal view of the stage. “Then a few people down the front want a slightly better view, so they stand. And, because of the crappy design of the stadium, it forces everybody else behind them to stand up. So now everyone’s standing, no one’s got a better view than before.” There’s no overall benefit. In fact, everyone is worse off, including those near the front: for the next few hours, they’ll have to stand if they want to see the stage. From a god's-eye view, everyone should remain sat down. From within the system, no one can stop a few people from acting from rational self-interest by standing up, so everyone must follow-suit.

This is also what happened when two people in a household got jobs instead of just the one: a few people acted on their rational self-interest (to increase their standard of living) > they gained a competitive advantage over the population (increased household income) > everyone follows suit, lest they get left behind (two-income household becamethe norm) > long-term detriment to the population (cost of living eventually increased for everyone) > everyone is worse off (two people working instead of one for the same standard of living).

Scott Alexander gives various examples of Moloch Traps in his prophetic 2014 blog post Meditations on Moloch (see Meditations on Moloch).

We can use Moloch Traps perfectly explain and predict the problem at hand: AI's impact on the economy at the individual- and company-level:
Individual: the few who adopt AI are more valuable in the labour market. they gain a short-term competitive advantage over the rest of the population who do not adopt AI. so, the rest of the population must follow suit and adopt AI, lest they get left behind. long-term, everyone is worse off, managing an AI and alienated from their work (some will enjoy this, most won't: turns out people hate AI).
You might think we just swap "people" with "company" and voila. But that's not where I'm going.
Company: the few companies who replace their workforce with AI can reduce prices to capture more of the market share. they gain a short-term competitive advantage over the rest of the companies who do not adopt AI. so, the rest of the companies must follow suit and replace their workforce with AI, lest they get left behind. long-term, companies are worse off as they are disempowered by something that probably doesn't align with their best interests (see ethics section).

• happens quicker at the individual level than the corporate level: smaller systems cam move faster. first we will see under hiring (mainly hitting grads, this is already happening) then increasing layoffs thereafter as impacts show up on bottom lines (customers going to companies with less employees and cheaper alternatives).
• this leads to deflation for both individuals and companies > excellent if you still have income and benefit from lower prices, not so excellent if you don't and can't buy anything. (Note: this is one of the arguments against "if no one has a job, who will buy all the stuff?" Things are not so black and white. This is what is called a K-shaped economy: the have's and the have-not's).
